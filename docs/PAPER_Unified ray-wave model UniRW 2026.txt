Unified ray-wave model for end-to-end imaging in refractive–diffractive hybrid optics
 
Abstract
End-to-end optical design has emerged as a promising paradigm in computational imaging, emphasizing the joint optimization of optical elements and computational algorithms. However, existing models struggle to simultaneously achieve efficiency, accuracy, and differentiability, which limits the development of refractive–diffractive hybrid systems. To address this challenge, we propose a unified ray-wave (UniRW) differentiable modeling framework that couples generalized Snell’s law with wave propagation. By unifying geometric ray tracing and wave optics, the framework enables accurate and efficient simulation of arbitrary hybrid optical systems, and can be naturally embedded into end-to-end optimization pipelines to support joint learning of optical parameters and image reconstruction networks. We validate the proposed framework on aberration correction and extended depth-of-field (EDOF) tasks. In aberration correction, the proposed hybrid lens achieves improved image reconstruction quality enabled by more accurate PSF modeling and end-to-end optimization. Quantitative results show that UniRW improves the reconstructed image peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) by approximately 3.4 dB. In the EDOF setting, the proposed framework achieves high-quality image reconstruction with a PSNR of approximately 31.9 dB across an extended axial defocus range of ±500µm. These results demonstrate that the proposed approach bridges physical modeling and deep learning optimization, providing an efficient, accurate, and general paradigm for hybrid optical design.

1. Introduction
In recent years, end-to-end optical design has emerged as a new paradigm in computational imaging and intelligent optical systems [1–5]. Unlike the traditional optical design [6–8]—which focuses on achieving optimal optical performance—end-to-end design emphasizes the joint optimization of the optical front end (encoder) and the computational back end (decoder). Within this framework, the optical system is no longer a passive imaging medium but an active encoder with information modulation capabilities, while deep neural networks can adaptively learn optimal decoding strategies to maximize overall system performance.
At the same time, the rise of hybrid refractive–diffractive optical systems (HROs) has introduced both opportunities and challenges [9,10]. On one hand, HROs combine the high transmission and imaging stability of refractive elements with the flexible phase modulation of diffractive elements, showing advantages in chromatic aberration correction [11–14], extended depth-of-field (EDOF) [15–18], and system miniaturization [16,19–23]. On the other hand, as system complexity grows, end-to-end design places increasingly strict demands on modeling methods in terms of accuracy, efficiency, and differentiability.
However, existing modeling paradigms cannot simultaneously satisfy these requirements. Geometric models (geometric ray tracing) [24–26] offer high computational efficiency but neglect wave phenomena such as EnM and interference. In contrast, wave optics models (e.g., Fresnel propagation [27], angular spectrum method(ASM) [28], Rayleigh–Sommerfeld integral [29]) capture complex EnM effects but suffer from high computational cost, limiting their applicability to large-scale simulation and hindering their direct integration into end-to-end optimization pipelines. Several novel tracing approaches have been proposed to alleviate this issue. For instance, Zhu et al [30] approximate wave propagation using ray-based formulation to improve efficiency but introduce approximation errors. Yang et al [31] reconstructs wavefronts from geometric rays, but constrains the system layout such that diffractive optical element(DOE) must follow refractive elements, limiting its applicability in general hybrid architectures. Nevertheless, these approaches either impose restrictions on system structure or suffer from rapidly increasing computational cost in wide-field imaging, and thus remain insufficient for arbitrary refractive–diffractive systems.
To overcome these limitations, we propose a unified Ray-wave (UniRW) differentiable framework that couples generalized Snell’s law [32] with differentiable wave propagation. As illustrated in Fig.  1 , light rays originating from the object plane sequentially traverse all optical elements in the system. When encountering a DOE, the exit angles are governed by the generalized Snell’s law. After passing through the final optical surface, the rays are reconstructed into an incident wavefront, which then propagates to the image plane to form the point spread function (PSF). This framework not only unifies the simulation of arbitrary refractive–diffractive hybrid systems, but also explicitly models the dependence of the output image on system parameters (including refractive lenses and diffractive phase elements). Similar ideas have also appeared in recent studies [33], where generalized Snell’s law and differentiable ray-tracing frameworks were employed for hybrid optics. Unlike Zhang et al., which focused on vectorial ray-based optimization of achromatic meta-optics, our UniRW framework explicitly reconstructs the wavefront from ray paths and propagates it to the image plane, thus capturing EnM and interference effects that their model neglects. Compared with this closely related work, our framework offers higher accuracy, broader applicability, and greater versatility.

We systematically evaluated the accuracy and generality of the proposed UniRW framework through two representative tasks: aberration correction and EDOF. In aberration correction, the reconstructed images achieved a peak signal-to-noise ratio (PSNR) of 39.73 dB; in EDOF extension, the system’s depth of focus was enlarged to 500  without sacrificing resolution. To the best of our knowledge, this is the first work that integrates the generalized Snell’s law into an end-to-end optical-design pipeline whose objective is the final image quality. Owing to its efficiency and scalability, the approach is readily extendable to large-field-of-view, multi-wavelength, and multi-scene imaging scenarios, offering a new solution to the long-standing efficiency-versus-accuracy dilemma in optical modeling. We envision that this study will provide a flexible and powerful design paradigm for future computational imaging systems.
2. Methods
2.1. UniRW model
Our optical model supports incident rays at arbitrary angles. Once the incident ray is explicitly defined, ray tracing can be performed as it enters the geometric lens. The essence of this process lies in solving for the unknown emergent ray based on the parameters of the known incident ray. In freeform optical systems, we sequentially compute the intersection point of each ray with a given lens surface, as well as the corresponding refracted ray. Specifically, for refractive elements, we employ Newton’s method to accurately determine the intersection point of the ray with each lens surface, while the direction of the refracted ray is obtained using Snell’s law, as described in earlier work [34–37].

From a theoretical standpoint, the superior computational efficiency of the proposed UniRW model arises from its hybrid formulation that unifies geometric ray tracing and wave-based propagation within a single differentiable framework. Traditional wave optics models, such as Fresnel or angular-spectrum propagation, require dense sampling across the entire pupil and multiple Fourier transforms, leading to a computational complexity that scales quadratically with the aperture size. In contrast, UniRW reconstructs the wavefront directly from sparse ray-tracing results, where each ray serves as a carrier of phase and amplitude information. This ray-guided reconstruction avoids repeated 2D EnM propagation and significantly reduces redundant spatial sampling. Collectively, these characteristics enable the UniRW framework to achieve near–ray-tracing-level runtime while preserving wave-level accuracy, effectively resolving the long-standing trade-off between efficiency and fidelity in hybrid optical modeling.

3. Results
3.1. Model validation
We first constructed a simplified optical system consisting of two convex lenses with focal lengths of 16 mm and 101 mm, together with a DOE, as illustrated in Fig.  4 . Based on this setup, we compared the simulated PSFs obtained from our model with the Huygens PSFs generated by the commercial software Zemax, and further benchmarked our results against the method of Zhang et al. [33]. As can be clearly observed, the PSFs predicted by our model are highly consistent with the Zemax Huygens PSFs, demonstrating superior accuracy. In contrast, the approach of Zhang et al. neglect EnM effects, resulting in less accurate PSFs, which in turn undermines the precision of subsequent end-to-end optimization.
As shown in Table  1 , we quantitatively compare the computational efficiency of UniRW-Spatial (ours) and UniRW-ASM (ours) with a conventional ASM. Although all methods are based on wave optics propagation, they differ substantially in numerical realization. The conventional ASM performs FFT-based propagation on a dense global grid determined by the system aperture, whereas UniRW directly generates the local PSF patches required for image formation. In all compared experiments, the optical element surfaces are sampled at a resolution of 3000  3000, while the image-plane PSFs are evaluated on local 65  65 patches, with a uniform pixel size of 4 . Runtime and peak GPU memory usage are reported for a single forward pass that simultaneously computes PSFs for all wavelengths and fields under identical sampling and output settings. These results validate that the proposed UniRW framework significantly reduces computational cost in practical configurations, while enabling flexible trade-offs between different wave optics backends.

3.2. Aberration correction
We first conducted simulation-based design of the end-to-end hybrid lens, with the primary objective of leveraging a DOE to correct aberrations in the optical system. In our modeling, the designed system had a numerical aperture (NA) of 0.2 and a focal length of 17 mm. Considering the rotational symmetry of the optical system, we exploited this property to select a small set of image heights  in polar coordinates for PSF evaluation. Although full sampling of the PSF across the Cartesian image plane  is possible, such an approach would incur prohibitive memory costs. To reduce storage requirements, we computed the PSF only within localized image patches , where  and  define the local origin of the sampled coordinates  [24,26,36].
The proposed framework was implemented and trained on a workstation equipped with an NVIDIA RTX 4060 Ti GPU (8 GB memory). Notably, training the model for 100 epochs required only approximately 3.5 hours, which verifies the advantages of our model in terms of low computational resource requirements and high time efficiency.
To comprehensively evaluate the proposed UniRW framework for aberration correction, we compare it against two widely used baselines—namely the paraxial wave optics model and the Zemax ray-tracing model—as well as two representative recent state-of-the-art hybrid modeling approaches proposed by Zhang et al [33] and Yang et al. [31](Fig.  5 .). The design and evaluation workflows for each are summarized as follows. For the paraxial wave optics model (Fig.  5 (a)), all optical elements were represented as phase masks. During the design stage, we precomputed the on-axis chromatic aberration of the refractive lens and designed a DOE for chromatic correction. The imaging results were then simulated using the proposed framework. For the Zemax ray-tracing model (Fig.  5 (b)), the hybrid lens was first imported into Zemax, where the DOE was modeled using the "Binary 2" surface. The refractive lens and DOE were jointly optimized with the root-mean-square (RMS) spot size as the objective, and the resulting optimized lens profiles are shown as orange dashed curves. This Zemax-optimized hybrid system was then used as a common starting point and imported into other framework. Because the method proposed by Yang et al. [31] does not support explicitly incorporating a physical refractive lens after the DOE element in the optical system, we replaced the final aspheric refractive surface following the DOE with an ideal thin lens as an equivalent surrogate to complete the imaging propagation. In contrast, the method of Zhang et al. [33] is formulated as a purely vectorial geometric ray-tracing model based on the generalized Snell’s law.

And (iii) a hybrid lens system integrated with a DOE optimized via UniRW model. For each configuration, we simulated the corresponding image datasets and trained a dedicated image reconstruction network, enabling effective restoration of the captured images to optimal quality. The trained networks were then applied to reconstruct the simulated images, followed by comprehensive quantitative and qualitative analyses.

In summary, these results demonstrate that the DOE optimized by the proposed UniRW enables effective DOF extension while maintaining high image quality after reconstruction. The advantage over conventional cubic phase EDOF designs originates from a fundamental difference in optimization strategy. Traditional cubic phase plates are typically designed to enforce a depth-invariant imaging response, which is effective for moderate DOF extensions on the order of  [42]. However, when targeting a substantially larger range such as , such designs often lead to imaging responses that are difficult to robustly invert, resulting in degraded reconstruction quality.
In contrast, the proposed UniRW directly optimizes the reconstructed image quality at different depths in an end-to-end manner, without enforcing depth-invariant behavior. By jointly considering physical wave propagation and computational decoding, the optimized DOE achieves imaging responses that remain well-conditioned for reconstruction across a large depth range. This paradigm shift toward image-driven, end-to-end optimization explains the superior EDOF performance achieved by the proposed method.
4. Discussion
The proposed UniRW framework combines the complementary strengths of geometric ray tracing and wave optics propagation within a unified and differentiable modeling pipeline. By using ray tracing to efficiently capture system geometry and optical path variations, and wave propagation to model EnM and interference effects, UniRW enables accurate simulation of refractive–diffractive hybrid optical systems at a computational cost suitable for end-to-end optimization. In addition, the modular structure of the framework allows refractive elements, diffractive optical elements, and freeform surfaces to be flexibly integrated, making UniRW particularly practical for simulating and optimizing contemporary hybrid optical architectures.
The limitations of UniRW primarily originate from its underlying physical assumptions. The current formulation adopts a scalar optical model, which may limit its applicability in scenarios involving strong vectorial electromagnetic effects, complex polarization-dependent interactions, or illumination conditions that deviate significantly from these assumptions. Furthermore, phenomena dominated by near-field coupling, resonant interactions, or detailed energy redistribution among multiple EnM orders are not explicitly resolved and are treated only through effective approximations.
Finally, the present work focuses on simulation-based modeling and optimization. Experimental validation, including DOE fabrication, system assembly and alignment, as well as robustness analysis under model–experiment mismatch, will be the subject of future work and is expected to further assess the practical performance of the proposed framework. In addition, we plan to extend the current image-based formulation to holographic imaging scenarios by incorporating more realistic source characteristics [44] into the UniRW framework.
5. Conclusion
In this work, we propose a novel differentiable hybrid optical modeling framework that combines high efficiency with high accuracy, addressing the long-standing trade-off among efficiency, precision, and differentiability in computational imaging. By coupling geometric ray tracing with wave propagation based on the generalized Snell’s law, we construct a unified framework capable of accurately simulating arbitrary combinations of refractive–diffractive hybrid optical systems while providing the necessary gradient information for end-to-end optical design.
We validated the performance and generality of the proposed model through two key tasks. In the aberration-correction task, we achieve, for the first time, end-to-end optimization of hybrid diffractive–refractive lenses based on the generalized Snell’s law. Experimental results demonstrate that, compared with the other baseline models, the hybrid lenses designed using the UniRW framework exhibit significant advantages in aberration correction.
Furthermore, in the EDOF task, we optimized an EDOF DOE using the proposed model. Quantitative and qualitative comparisons with conventional optical systems and a classical cubic phase EDOF design confirm that the proposed hybrid system can effectively extend the imaging system’s depth of field while maintaining high resolution.
Beyond efficiency and accuracy, the proposed UniRW framework also exhibits strong generality and modularity. Its unified mathematical formulation allows flexible integration of refractive, diffractive, and freeform optical elements within the same differentiable pipeline. Each optical component is modeled as an independent, parameterized module governed by generalized Snell’s law or wave propagation, enabling straightforward extension to complex hybrid and multi-wavelength systems.
In summary, the proposed differentiable optical model successfully bridges the gap between physical modeling and deep learning optimization. It provides an efficient, accurate, and generalizable tool for optical design, and opens a broad design space with strong technical support for future trainable optical systems in applications such as miniaturized microscopes, where physical size constraints and stringent performance requirements are critical.
